พระเครื่อง

Phra Khrueang

Buddhist amulets

The general term for Buddhist amulets: small sacred images, commonly of the Buddha or a revered monk, carried or worn.

Everyday usage: In an amulet conversation, people often shorten this to พระ (Phra). The full term makes the meaning explicit; outside this context, Phra can refer to a monk or a sacred image.

Hear Thai online ↗ See pictures ↗
Explore meaning & tradition

The main Buddhist-amulet term. Phra khrueang is the key general term for Buddhist amulets, commonly small sacred images carried or worn. It is broader than the name of a single model such as Phra Somdet or Phra Pit Ta, but more specific than the English word “amulet” used for charms of many kinds.

Forms and materials. Thai historical writing connects worn Buddhist amulets with moulded votive tablets deposited in stupas. Clay, powder mixtures, and cast metal are important distinctions between forms. An image’s design and its material can therefore be named separately.

Useful neighbouring words. Phra phim emphasizes a moulded image or tablet; rup lo emphasizes a cast image; rian identifies a medal. Phra bucha usually identifies an image intended for an altar. These terms help you recognize what kind of sacred object is being discussed.

Related: Phra Phim · Phra Bucha · Watthu Mongkhon

Umbrella term · Foundation

ของขลัง

Khong Khlang

Sacred objects

Objects regarded as possessing sacred or protective power.

Hear Thai online ↗ See pictures ↗
Explore meaning & tradition

A broad starting term. Khong khlang is a broad expression for things regarded as possessing sacred or protective power. It names their perceived potency, rather than a particular shape or material. A worn amulet, an inscribed object, and a naturally unusual substance can all be discussed within this larger field.

Historical classification. Phya Anuman Rajadhon’s 1964 account distinguishes naturally potent things, Buddhist amulets, ritually empowered objects, and magical plants. This is a historical framework for understanding the range of objects, rather than a rule that every community uses the same categories.

Reading related terms. Phra khrueang points more specifically toward Buddhist amulets. Khrueang rang means charms. Watthu mongkhon emphasizes an object’s auspicious or sacred status. These expressions overlap in use; their emphasis helps explain what a speaker is describing.

Related: Khrueang Rang · Watthu Mongkhon · Yant

Umbrella term · Foundation

เครื่องราง

Khrueang Rang

Charms

A charm carried, worn, or kept for protection or good fortune.

Hear Thai online ↗ See pictures ↗
Explore meaning & tradition

A broad object category. Khrueang rang is a general word for charms: objects carried, worn, or kept in connection with protection or good fortune. The word identifies a broad kind of object. It does not specify whether that object is a shell, fang, carved figure, inscribed scroll, or another form.

What a description adds. A charm’s name may be followed by its material, its maker, and the purpose attributed to it. Those are different pieces of information. Takrut describes a rolled inscribed charm, while metta maha niyom describes an aim associated with goodwill and popularity.

Connection to Buddhist amulets. English speakers often use “amulet” for this entire field. Thai vocabulary allows a more specific distinction between a charm and phra khrueang, a Buddhist amulet. The distinction is useful without treating the categories as completely separate in every conversation.

Related: Khong Khlang · Watthu Mongkhon · Takrut

Umbrella term · Foundation

วัตถุมงคล

Watthu Mongkhon

Auspicious sacred objects

A broad term for objects regarded as sacred or auspicious, including amulets and charms.

Hear Thai online ↗ See pictures ↗

Related: Khong Khlang · Khrueang Rang · Phra Khrueang

Explore meaning & tradition

An umbrella expression. Watthu means an object or thing; mongkhon expresses auspiciousness. Together, watthu mongkhon is a broad expression for objects regarded as auspicious or sacred. It is useful when talking about a range of items without naming one particular object type.

How it relates to other names. A Buddhist amulet can be called both phra khrueang and watthu mongkhon. An inscribed charm may be named by its form, such as takrut, and also described as an auspicious object. The broad expression and the specific object name answer different questions.

What the label tells you. This name concerns the object’s cultural or devotional status. Its shape, ingredients, edition, maker, and associated beliefs still need their own explanation. It provides an entry point into the vocabulary rather than a complete description of an individual item.

พระปิดตา

Phra Pit Ta

Closed-eyes amulet

An amulet showing a seated figure covering its eyes.

Hear Thai online ↗ See pictures ↗
Explore meaning & tradition

Recognizing the image. The familiar Phra Pit Ta form shows a seated figure covering the eyes. The gesture is central to recognizing the amulet, but figures sold under this broad name need not share identical poses, materials, or ritual interpretations.

The nine-opening form. Thai accounts distinguish a Maha Ut form in which additional hands seal bodily openings, traditionally counted as nine. This connects the image with weapon-stopping protection. It is a specific iconographic explanation rather than a description that should be applied to every closed-eyes figure.

Related vocabulary. Phra Pit Ta names an image type; maha ut names an attributed protective power. Keeping those two levels distinct helps explain why a design, an amulet, and a tattoo can share protective associations without being the same kind of object.

หุ่นพยนต์

Hun Phayon

Guardian effigy

A human-shaped effigy consecrated as a guardian.

Hear Thai online ↗ See pictures ↗
Explore meaning & tradition

A guardian in human form. Hun phayon is a human-shaped effigy ritually consecrated as a guardian. The important features are its human form and its role in a particular ritual tradition. Thai accounts connect it with guarding a person, a household, or property.

Material and ritual identity. An effigy’s material is a separate question from its name. Likewise, the explanation of how it is animated or consecrated belongs to its maker’s tradition. The word hun phayon should not be treated as a recipe naming one mandatory ingredient.

A useful distinction. This differs from a kuman thong, whose child-spirit identity and care relationship are central to its description. Both may be associated with protecting a home, but a shared purpose does not erase the difference between the objects and their traditions.

Inscribed charms and ritual implements

ตะกรุด

Takrut

Rolled scroll charm

A rolled scroll charm, commonly made from a thin metal sheet inscribed with sacred letters or diagrams.

Hear Thai online ↗ See pictures ↗
Explore meaning & tradition

Form and inscription. A takrut begins with a writing surface, commonly a thin metal sheet, that is inscribed with sacred characters or diagrams and rolled into a small tube. Some descriptions also record palm-leaf forms. The rolled form gives this charm a different appearance from a flat yantra cloth or a moulded Buddhist tablet.

Carrying and wearing. Historical Thai accounts describe scroll charms strung on cords and worn at the waist as well as carried on the body. The inscription, material, and teacher’s tradition give an individual takrut its identity. Its shape alone does not explain the particular aim attributed to it.

Understanding its name. A description can combine the object name with a purpose or material. The word takrut identifies the scroll; a term such as maha sane describes attraction. This is the same distinction between physical form and attributed purpose that is useful when reading tattoo names.

Related: Yant · Khatha · Lek Chan · Phitsamon

ผ้ายันต์

Pha Yant

Yantra cloth

Cloth bearing sacred diagrams or inscriptions.

Hear Thai online ↗ See pictures ↗
Explore meaning & tradition

The sacred diagram on cloth. Pha yant names the surface as well as the inscription: a cloth bearing yantra diagrams, numerals, or sacred script. It can be displayed in a house or shop, folded and carried, or tied to the body. Its physical form makes it easier to recognize than a purpose word alone.

Regional purposes. Writing on Lanna yantra cloths records aims including auspiciousness, luck, goodwill, protection from spirits, protection from weapons, and protection from other dangers. These are classifications of attributed use. They do not imply that every cloth bears the same diagram or is used in the same way.

Connection to tattoos. Yant names the sacred diagram. Pha yant places it on cloth; sak yant places sacred diagrams or inscriptions on the body. The shared word explains the relationship between these practices while the complete names identify different physical forms.

Related: Yant · Yant Paet Thit

มีดหมอ

Mit Mo

Ritual knife

A ritual knife used in Thai magical practice.

Hear Thai online ↗ See pictures ↗
Explore meaning & tradition

A working ritual implement. Mit mo is a ritual knife associated with Thai magical practice. It may bear sacred writing, and historical descriptions connect it with protective and healing rites, including rites concerned with harmful magic or spirits. Its role is understood through the practitioner’s tradition.

Object and use. A ritual knife can function as an implement used during a ceremony and also as a protective object carried or kept. Those uses help explain why it appears alongside both ritual vocabulary and the names of charms.

Reading an individual description. The maker, inscription, material, and tradition identify a particular knife. The general name supplies the object category. A claimed effect or a story about one teacher is additional information, not part of the physical definition of every mit mo.

Child-spirit figures and funerary charms

กุมารทอง

Kuman Thong

Golden child spirit

A child-spirit figure kept in a relationship of care, offerings, and expected assistance.

Hear Thai online ↗ See pictures ↗
Explore meaning & tradition

Historical and literary accounts. Accounts of kuman thong include a fetus taken from a woman who died while pregnant, dried by fire, and lacquered and gilded. This belongs to recorded literary and traditional material. It is important background for the term, alongside the contemporary devotional forms people encounter.

Contemporary forms and care. Present-day figures can be made from powders, herbs, or other materials. Keepers may offer food, drinks, and toys, treating the spirit as a child. The relationship is described in terms of care and expected assistance, such as guarding the household or bringing good fortune.

Related but distinct terms. Kuman thong names the golden child-spirit tradition. Prai kuman refers to a child prai spirit, while rak yom names a pair of child figures and luk krok has its own folkloric meaning. The terms are connected, but the figure’s appearance alone does not make them interchangeable.

Related: Prai Kuman · Luk Krok · Phi Tai Thang Klom

รักยม

A pair of small child figures traditionally made from two kinds of wood, rak and mayom.

Hear Thai online ↗ See pictures ↗
Explore meaning & tradition

Why there are two figures. Rak yom names a paired child-spirit form. The Thai dictionary connects the names to rak and mayom, the two woods traditionally used for the figures. This material explanation is part of understanding the name, not just a decorative feature of the pair.

Care and devotion. Popular Thai accounts describe keeping the figures with fragrant oil and offering food or other items. Such descriptions explain a devotional relationship associated with the pair. The exact materials and observances belong to the example and tradition being described.

Related vocabulary. Rak yom, kuman thong, and prai kuman all appear in discussions of child-spirit traditions. Rak yom is useful as its own entry because it identifies the pair and its specific naming tradition, rather than simply another spelling for kuman thong.

Khom script

Hear Thai online ↗ See pictures ↗
Explore meaning & tradition

A writing system. Akson Khom refers to a Khmer-derived writing tradition used in Thai religious manuscripts and sacred diagrams. It names the script: the visual system of characters. It is useful vocabulary for recognizing writing on tattoos and inscribed objects.

Script is not language. A text written in Khom can be Pali. The script answers “what characters are these?” while the language answers “what language is written here?” Keeping the questions separate avoids assuming that every Khom inscription is a Khmer-language sentence.

Connected terms. Akkhara names letters or characters; khatha names a verse or incantation; yant names a sacred diagram. A single inscription may involve all three. Different regional practices also use other scripts, including Lanna Tham.

Protection and avoiding danger

แคล้วคลาด

Khlaeo Khlat

Avoidance of danger

Escaping or avoiding danger.

Hear Thai online ↗
Explore meaning & tradition

Danger that does not reach you. Khlaeo khlat expresses avoidance of danger: an accident or attack misses, is escaped, or does not reach the person. This is different from a story in which a blade strikes someone but fails to injure an allegedly invulnerable body.

Why the distinction matters. English descriptions may group several Thai purposes under “protection.” Separating avoidance, bodily invulnerability, and weapon-stopping protection helps a reader understand the particular claim associated with a charm or tattoo.

Reading across the glossary. The name of a physical object, such as takrut, identifies its form. Khlaeo khlat identifies an aim attributed to it. The same aim can appear in accounts of different objects and practices, so it belongs among purposes rather than one exclusive object type.

คงกระพันชาตรี

Invulnerability

A traditional expression for protection from weapons and bodily harm.

Hear Thai online ↗
Explore meaning & tradition

An attributed condition of the body. Khong kraphan describes bodily resistance to weapons in Thai accounts of protective magic. A Thai cultural account distinguishes it from chatri, associated with freedom from pain and a light body. The combined expression names a field of protective practice, not one particular object.

Several physical forms. Accounts discuss tattoos, amulets, oils, and implanted objects within this tradition. This explains why the same purpose vocabulary appears in both tattoo and amulet descriptions: the attributed aim can be shared even when the medium differs.

Compare the protective words. Maha ut concerns stopping a weapon’s effect. Khlaeo khlat concerns avoiding the danger. Reading these distinctions gives more information than translating all three simply as “protection.”

มหาอุด

Maha Ut

A traditional protective power said to prevent a weapon from functioning.

Hear Thai online ↗
Explore meaning & tradition

What the protective idea describes. Maha ut is associated with stopping a weapon’s effect: Thai accounts illustrate it with guns failing to fire or weapons failing to harm. These are descriptions within protective-magic traditions. The term identifies the kind of protection being claimed.

How it differs from neighbouring terms. Khong kraphan describes attributed bodily resistance to injury; khlaeo khlat describes avoiding danger or having it miss the person. The three can occur together in accounts of charms and tattoos, but they explain different protective ideas.

Recognizing the symbolism. The Maha Ut form of Phra Pit Ta seals bodily openings, traditionally counted as nine. This provides a visual connection to the protective term. It is useful to distinguish the symbolic image from the general purpose word.
